Part I

Meta-epistemologyThe analytical lens

There is a common formal ground shared by all knowledge.

Different fields study different things with different methods. But in every field, knowing something requires the same: telling apart, connecting, putting into words, seeing from a standpoint, and something being made real.

The study of knowledge asks what we can know, and how a claim can be justified. Here a question is asked that comes first: what must already be made real for there to be any knowledge, justification, description, disagreement, measurement or claim at all? It is the question of the preconditions of knowledge.

The answer is not a new theory competing with physics, biology or history. It is a description of the form every field already uses when it knows anything. That is why questions from very different fields can be examined on the same ground, without turning everything into one field.

Disciplines differ in content, method and object. But all of them require the same in order to know: distinctions, relations, concepts, perspective and actualization – something actually being made real.

Epistemology asks what can be known, and how a claim can be justified. Meta-epistemology asks what must already be actualized for there to be knowledge, justification, description, disagreement, measurement or claims at all.

This is no new theory competing with physics, biology or history, but an account of the formal structure the disciplines already use. That is why questions from different fields can be examined at a common level, without reducing their content to a single science.

Disciplines differ in content, method and object. They do not differ in whether knowing requires distinctions, relations, conceptual articulation, perspective and actualization.

Epistemology asks what can be known and how a claim can be justified. Meta-epistemology asks the prior question: what must already be actualized for there to be knowledge, justification, description, disagreement, measurement or a claim at all.

The epistemological reset

Speculative metaphysics cannot serve as ground.

A claim that can neither be checked by observation nor proven by pure logic cannot carry knowledge. That is the problem with the idea that the world consists of hidden, finished things behind what we see – "bearers" or "substrates" that properties and connections are supposed to rest on. No one can check them, yet they are treated as the ground.

The answer is not to put another such idea in their place. The unchecked ground is withdrawn. Instead, we examine the preconditions that are already made real wherever there is knowledge.

A claim that can neither be validated empirically nor justified as logical necessity cannot carry systematic knowledge. That is the problem with speculative metaphysics of substance: it posits hidden bearers or substrates behind things, and treats them as the ground of the properties and relations we actually know.

The answer is not to set one speculative metaphysics against another. The unwarranted ground is withdrawn, and meta-epistemology takes its place: the study of the conditions already actualized wherever knowledge occurs.

A claim that can neither be validated empirically nor anchored as pure logical necessity lacks the warrant required to carry systematic knowledge. This is the difficulty with speculative metaphysical substance ontology: it posits hidden bearers, substrates or independently subsisting things, and then treats them as the ground of the properties and relations that are actually available to knowledge.

The response is not to answer one speculative metaphysics with another. The unwarranted ground is withdrawn, and formal meta-epistemology takes its place: the study of the conditions already actualized wherever knowledge occurs.

The analytical instrument · the continuity axiom

Distinction is relation.

To tell apart is already to connect.

When you tell something apart from something else, you connect them at the same time. A distinction that connected nothing would tell nothing apart. The two sides do not first exist as finished things that are then linked; they become sides precisely through the telling apart that connects them.

This is a point about how we think and know – not a claim that only connections exist. Connection must not be turned into a new "thing".

To distinguish is already to relate.

This is the analytical instrument. A distinction that related nothing would not distinguish. The two poles of a distinction are not finished things that are connected afterwards – they become available as poles through the distinguishing relation itself.

The point is analytical, not a doctrine that only relations exist. Relation must not be made into a new substance.

Discretization, continuity, substantialism

The gap belongs to the distinction, not independently to what is distinguished.

To think, we must tell apart. Making something thinkable means dividing it into separate parts: drawing a boundary, or giving something an identity stable enough to think with. That is necessary, and telling apart is not the mistake. The mistake is forgetting that the boundary was drawn, and believing it existed by itself.

Because a distinction always connects its sides, the distinction alone can never create an absolute break between them. The gap belongs to the distinction, not to the things in themselves. This is not mathematics, not a hidden "substrate", and no claim about what physics will find.

The remedy is not to stop using nouns, but to keep the connection visible – also for the framework's own words.

Objectivity

Objectivity, in three modes.

Formal objectivity is perfect. It belongs to pure logic: the conclusion is already in the starting point, and analysis only makes it visible. When logic is used to describe something outside us, this perfect certainty is lost. But when we examine what already lies in our own thinking and acting, it is kept – because in I am, the one who knows and what is known are one and the same.

Experiential objectivity is conditional. It belongs to knowledge of the world around us, and depends on the concepts we meet the world with. That is why it must be checkable by others, through observation and repetition.

Ultimate objectivity – knowing something from no standpoint at all – is impossible. Knowing requires a standpoint, a standpoint requires telling apart, and to tell apart is to connect. This impossible limit shows what objectivity actually requires.

Formal, perfect objectivity belongs to analytic knowledge: the conclusions already lie in the premises. When a formal system is used to model something external, the application loses this certainty. Meta-epistemology is different: it analyses what already lies in the thinking and acting through which the analysis itself is actualized. In first-person certainty – I am – the knower and the known coincide, so validity is preserved.

Empirical, contingent objectivity belongs to knowledge of external phenomena. It is conditioned by the concepts we meet phenomena with, and must be intersubjectively verifiable through observation and repetition.

Ultimate, impossible objectivity is a perspective without perspective. Knowledge requires perspective, perspective requires distinction, and distinction is relation. Ultimate objectivity is therefore not a higher degree, but the limit that makes the conditions of objectivity visible.

Formal, perfect objectivity belongs to analytical knowledge. Its conclusions are already implicit and latent in the premises; analysis makes explicit what was already contained within them. No new empirical content is added and no external referent is approximated. When a formal system is used to model an external phenomenon, however, its axioms approximate that referent and its application loses apodictic validity. Meta-epistemology is different: it applies formal analysis to what is already implicit in the thinking and acting through which the analysis itself is actualized. In first-person certainty — I am — knower and known coincide in the same act. The referent is therefore not external and is not approximated, so apodictic validity is retained in application.

Empirical, contingent objectivity belongs to knowledge of external phenomena. Its objectivity is conditioned by the cognitive categories and conceptual frameworks through which phenomena become available to knowledge. Valid empirical knowledge must therefore be intersubjectively verifiable through observation and repetition. Without such validation, a claim is not empirical knowledge: it is either a formal proposition whose validity is analytical, or speculation about what lies beyond possible validation.

Ultimate, impossible objectivity is the limiting concept of a perspectiveless perspective. It is epistemically impossible. Knowledge requires perspective; perspective requires distinction; and distinction is relation. There can therefore be no knowledge from outside every relation and every perspective. Ultimate objectivity is not a higher degree of objectivity that inquiry might eventually attain, but the impossible limit against which the conditions of objectivity become visible.

Genuine and borrowed language

Ontonomy. Noonomy. Teleonomy.

When we describe something from outside, we always borrow words that really belong to the I: "I am", "I think" and "I act". The dog is hungry. The computer works and thinks. The flower turns toward the sun.

Each borrowing has a name. Ontonomy is lending "I am" to give something a stable identity. Noonomy is lending "I think" to understand how something processes or computes. Teleonomy is lending "I act" to understand what function something has.

Such borrowing is always necessary, and not wrong in itself. The error is reading the borrowing as though the description itself turned what it describes into an I that is, thinks or acts.

When we describe the world in the third person, we always borrow the language of being, meaning and action. The electron is in superposition. The computer processes information. Natural selection selects the fittest.

The framework names the borrowings: ontonomy borrows "I am" to stabilize identity, noonomy borrows "I think" to understand processing and computation, and teleonomy borrows "I act" to understand function. These borrowings are always indispensable and not errors in themselves. The error is reading the borrowed language as though the description itself established being, meaning or aims as they are actualized in the first person.

The action axiom

Human action is purposeful behavior.

I act, recognized in another: you act.

I have direct access to the fact that I act. When I meet another as you, you act becomes available – and the fundamental insight about action puts this into words. Whoever seriously denies it is themselves acting toward an aim.

Action is behaviour guided by an aim. It is not about visible movement: thinking, choosing, planning, weighing an objection or holding an aim up against the situation is also action. The opposite of action is not irrational action, but behaviour that merely reacts or adapts, without an aim.

Making theory is also purposeful action: one tells apart, connects, puts into words, tests consequences, answers objections and seeks a conclusion. A theory of action is therefore already an example of what it is about.

Value arises with the one who experiences: valuing is a connection made real by a being directed toward something. Action is rational in form because action is connecting means to an aim. Aims themselves are neither rational nor irrational; whether the means actually reach the aim is a question of the ability to have an effect.

Value, rationality and efficacy

Value is constituted at the subject pole: to value is a relation actualized by a purposeful being. That values are subjective is itself an objective structural fact, and it can therefore serve as a starting point for formal analysis rather than as an obstacle to it.

Action is rational in form because action is the relating of means to an end. Ends are neither rational nor irrational. Rationality lies in the means–end relation; whether the chosen means actually achieve the end is a question of efficacy.

Social reality

Converging narrative structures.

Society rests on our recognizing one another as "the other I", through shared ways of understanding reality through language. This makes it possible to talk together and cooperate on a large scale. Money, institutions, roles, laws and norms make sense because such understandings are shared enough. This does not mean society is made up – it shows what makes something a social phenomenon at all.

Recognizing another as an I also opens up ethics: the other can be met as one who experiences, with aims of their own that matter – or be treated merely as a means to one's own aims.

Society and civilization rest on mutual recognition of Alter Ego through converging narrative structures – shared understanding that makes communication and coordination possible at scale. Money, institutions, roles, legal categories and norms become intelligible within such shared structures of meaning. This does not mean society is a fiction; it identifies the formal condition that makes something available as a social phenomenon.

The Institute

Why the Institute exists

The Institute of Teleosophy — Teleosofisk Institutt — is a registered Norwegian non-profit. It was established to preserve, articulate, develop and make available a body of formal knowledge whose validity does not depend on personal or institutional authority.

The Institute exists because knowledge is fragile. Work that exists only in a limited set of documents or systems can be lost. The purpose is to give the corpus continuity beyond its original articulation, and to place it where others can inspect it, use it, derive from it and criticize it.

The ongoing work comprises this website, the wiki, and continued formal analysis and articulation of the implications of the meta-epistemological ground.
